The Science of Creatine: A Plain-English Guide

Published May 2025 · 8 min read · Science

Creatine is one of the most studied supplements in the world, with hundreds of peer-reviewed papers spanning more than four decades. Yet it's also one of the most misunderstood. This article breaks down what creatine actually is, what it does inside your body, and why creatine monohydrate remains the gold standard.

What is creatine?

Creatine is a naturally occurring compound made from three amino acids: arginine, glycine, and methionine. Your body produces about 1 to 2 grams per day, mostly in the liver and kidneys, and stores roughly 95% of it in your skeletal muscles. You also get small amounts from food — primarily red meat and fish.

How creatine works

Muscle cells use a molecule called adenosine triphosphate (ATP) as their primary energy source. During short bursts of intense activity — lifting a heavy weight, sprinting, jumping — ATP gets broken down for energy faster than your body can replenish it. Creatine, stored as phosphocreatine, donates a phosphate group to quickly regenerate ATP, helping you sustain effort and recover between sets.

In simple terms: more creatine in your muscles means more rapid energy availability, which translates to small but consistent improvements in strength, power output, and the ability to do "one more rep."

Why creatine monohydrate?

You may see "newer" forms of creatine marketed as superior — creatine HCL, ethyl ester, buffered creatine, and so on. The research, however, is clear: creatine monohydrate remains the most extensively studied and proven form. It's also the most cost-effective, has the highest documented absorption, and has decades of safety data behind it. Other forms have generally failed to demonstrate meaningful advantages in head-to-head studies.

How much do you need?

The most commonly studied maintenance dose is 3 to 5 grams per day. Some protocols include a "loading phase" of around 20g per day (split into 4 doses) for 5 to 7 days to saturate muscle stores faster, followed by a maintenance dose. Loading is optional — you'll reach the same saturation in about 3 to 4 weeks with the maintenance dose alone.

When should you take it?

Timing matters less than consistency. Creatine works through saturation of your muscle stores, not through an acute pre-workout effect. Taking it daily — including rest days — is what produces results. Many people prefer to take it with a meal or post-workout simply because it's an easy habit to remember.

Benefits supported by research

  • Increased strength and power output during resistance training.
  • Improved performance in short, high-intensity activities (sprints, jumps, lifts).
  • Faster recovery between high-effort sets and sessions.
  • Lean mass support when combined with resistance training.
  • Emerging cognitive benefits, particularly under stress or sleep deprivation.

Safety

For healthy adults, creatine monohydrate has an excellent safety profile in the published literature. It does not cause kidney damage in healthy individuals at standard doses, despite a persistent myth to the contrary. As with any supplement, talk to your healthcare provider before starting, particularly if you have an existing medical condition.

The bottom line

Creatine isn't a miracle compound. It's a well-understood, well-tolerated supplement with a strong evidence base for modest but real improvements in strength, power, and recovery — and a growing list of additional potential benefits.
