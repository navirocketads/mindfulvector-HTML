Creatine and the Brain: Beyond the Gym

Published March 2025 · 7 min read · Brain Health

Creatine is best known as a performance supplement, but a growing body of research is exploring its role in brain function. The story is interesting, the evidence is real but still developing, and the marketing is — predictably — sometimes ahead of the science.

Why creatine matters for the brain

Your brain is a major consumer of energy. It maintains its own stores of creatine and phosphocreatine to support rapid ATP regeneration in neurons. Lower brain creatine has been observed in states of fatigue, sleep deprivation, certain depressive presentations, and some neurological conditions.

What the research shows

Several controlled studies have looked at creatine supplementation and cognitive performance. Findings, summarized:

  • Sleep deprivation: creatine supplementation has been shown to mitigate some cognitive deficits associated with poor sleep.
  • Mental fatigue: studies on demanding cognitive tasks suggest small but measurable improvements with supplementation, particularly in vegetarians or people with lower baseline creatine intake.
  • Aging: some research points to potential benefits for memory and processing speed in older adults.
  • Mood: exploratory studies have looked at creatine's potential role as an adjunct in depression — promising, but far from a clinical recommendation at this stage.

Important caveats

The brain takes up creatine more slowly than muscle, and studies often use doses or durations that are different from typical performance protocols. Effect sizes for cognitive outcomes are smaller and more variable than for strength. None of this is a substitute for sleep, nutrition, or — where applicable — appropriate medical treatment.

Who might benefit most

People who, on average, have lower baseline creatine stores tend to show larger responses to supplementation. This includes vegetarians and vegans (who don't get dietary creatine from meat and fish), older adults, and people in periods of poor sleep or high stress.

What this doesn't mean

Creatine is not a "smart drug." It won't transform a tired brain into a sharp one. It's not a substitute for sleep, exercise, social connection, or any of the boring foundations of brain health. What it appears to be is a modest, well-tolerated supplement with growing evidence of cognitive support — particularly when your system is under stress.

The takeaway

Creatine for the brain is one of the more interesting frontiers in supplement research. The evidence is real, the effects appear modest, and the safety profile is well-established. Combined with the well-documented physical benefits, it's a small, sensible addition to a daily routine for many people.
